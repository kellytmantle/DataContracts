# 01 — Why Data Contracts

## The problem

Large financial organisations accumulate vast amounts of data over years of platform evolution. Data is generated by operational systems, moved between platforms, and consumed by dozens — sometimes hundreds — of downstream teams and processes. Over time, this creates a complex web of implicit dependencies.

The core problem is this: **the teams that produce data and the teams that consume it have no formal agreement between them.** Data engineering teams make changes to schemas, pipelines, and data structures based on their own priorities and timelines. Downstream teams — those building dashboards, risk models, regulatory reports, and other data-driven products — find out about those changes when things break.

This is not a failure of intent. It is a failure of process and structure.

## What breaking changes look like in practice

When a data engineering team renames a column, changes a calculation, or removes an attribute from a table, the consequences are felt downstream — sometimes immediately, sometimes weeks later when a scheduled process runs. A typical failure chain looks like this:

1. A data engineer updates a schema to accommodate new business requirements.
2. The change is tested in isolation and deployed to production.
3. A downstream dashboard stops refreshing, or a risk model produces incorrect numbers.
4. An incident is raised. Engineers investigate and eventually trace the issue back to the schema change.
5. The data engineering team rolls back or patches. The downstream team remediates.

This cycle is expensive in engineering time, erodes trust between teams, and — in a regulated environment — can have compliance consequences.

## Why this problem gets worse at scale

The problem compounds when an organisation moves towards a more regionally aligned platform model, where data is shared across markets and consumed by many more downstream teams from shared sources. In this model, a single breaking change to a shared data product can simultaneously affect dozens of consumers in different business lines, different countries, and different regulatory jurisdictions.

At the same time, data platforms are expected to move quickly — keeping up with business requirements, regulatory change, and technology evolution. This creates a genuine tension: **the need to change data frequently, and the need to protect consumers from the effects of that change.**

Data contracts are how that tension is resolved.

## The status quo and its cost

Without data contracts, organisations typically manage change through informal communication: emails, Slack messages, word of mouth, or — at best — release notes that may or may not reach the right people. This approach has several well-documented failure modes:

- Changes are not communicated to all consumers, because the producer may not know who all of their consumers are.
- Consumers have no formal obligation to respond to change notices or to migrate within a given timeframe.
- There is no mechanism for consumers to communicate their quality requirements to producers.
- Producers have no visibility over whether their changes have caused downstream failures.
- The effort of managing change falls disproportionately on consumer teams, who often have no influence over the producer's timeline.

The result is that teams invest heavily in defensive measures: copying data into their own platforms, building redundant pipelines, and over-validating inputs — all to protect themselves from changes they cannot predict or control. This duplication is expensive, leads to inconsistency, and makes the overall data estate harder to manage.

## What we are trying to achieve

The goal of the data contract framework described in this documentation is to establish a formal, governed way of managing change to data in data platforms — one that allows data to evolve without breaking consumers, gives consumers confidence in the stability and quality of what they consume, and creates clear accountability on both sides.

This is not about slowing down change. It is about making change safe, predictable, and manageable at scale.

The principles we use are not new — they are borrowed directly from how the engineering community manages interfaces in software systems. The next section explains how.
