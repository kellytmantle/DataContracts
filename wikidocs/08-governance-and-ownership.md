# 08: Governance and Ownership

Data contracts and operating principles only work if someone is accountable for them. This section deals with the harder questions: who owns what, how do you make that ownership real, and how do you fund and govern data products across an organisation that wasn't designed with any of this in mind?

---

## Who owns a data product?

Ownership has two dimensions, business ownership and IT ownership, and they're often conflated.

**Business ownership** is accountability for the data itself: its accuracy, completeness, and fitness for purpose. The business owner should the power to fix data quality problems at source, by correcting processes in operational systems, implementing better input controls, resolving exceptions. A data product without a business owner empowered to fix data quality issues, problems get passed downstream to business and data engineering teams who have no authority to fix the root cause. They can clean and patch, but underlaying problem remains a problem for everyone. 

**IT ownership** is responsibility for translating the data product owner's requirements into a working technical implementation—ensuring the product runs on a platform that can actually meet its functional and non-functional requirements. If the contract commits to a specific SLA or has volume, latency, or availability requirements, those must be validated against what the platform can deliver. Day-to-day, IT ownership means ensuring pipeline logic executes correctly and on time, every time. The IT owner is accountable for execution quality, monitoring it continuously, and resolving failures before they become consumer-facing problems.

### The business owner should be the team that generates the data

The logic is straightforward. They're closest to the data, they understand it best, and they're the only ones with the authority to fix problems where they originate. If a field is being populated incorrectly in an operational system, that's where the fix needs to happen. No amount of downstream transformation addresses the root cause.

In some cases, the business teams are usually not the ones consuming the data they produce. The data generated by a trading desk or a lending operation is consumed primarily by risk, finance, and compliance, not by the team that created it. So why would they invest in improving its quality? Without the right governance and incentive structures, this becomes a problem. This is one of the central issues that a data contract framework has to solve.

## The Data Product Owner role

This section sets out what the role actually requires, what good owners care about, and how organisations can make the role worth doing well.

### What the role requires

A Data Product Owner is accountable for the quality, reliability, and fitness-for-purpose of one or more data products. It's not a passive role. It requires active engagement with the data, with consumers, and with the teams that build and maintain the data pipelines.

**Owning the contract.** The data contract is the public commitment your product makes to its consumers. You're responsible for keeping it accurate, updating it as the product evolves, and communicating changes clearly and in advance. A contract that's out of date can't be trusted, and a product that can't be trusted may not be used, or even wrose, is used and causes unwelcome outcomes. 

**Managing change.** Breaking changes can cause significant downstream disruption. As owner, you decide when they happen, how much notice consumers get, and whether exceptions are warranted. That means you need to know who your consumers are, not just as a registry entry, but well enough to understand the impact of your decisions on their work.

**Championing quality at source.** Quality problems are almost always cheaper to fix where they start. As business owner, you have the authority to influence the operational processes that generate the data. Raising issues with the right people, pushing back on process changes that would degrade quality, implementing better input controls: this is some of the most valuable work a business owner can do, and it's the work that gets skipped most often.

**Maintaining an inventory of defects.** You're accountable for a complete, current inventory of every known defect in your data product: data quality issues, schema problems, performance degradation, contract breaches, and any other gap between what the product delivers and what it promises. For each defect, document the remediation plan and target resolution date, tracked against your KPIs. This is a formal requirement under BCBS 239 frameworks, but it's a critical discipline regardless of regulatory scope. If a defect can't be resolved within your authority, escalate it with full context so the people with decision-making power can see what they're choosing to accept.

**Representing your consumers.** Consumers depend on your product and can't fix the problems they encounter. Part of your role is to understand how the product is being used, what expectations consumers have around quality and timeliness, and whether able to meeting them before onboarding. 

**Keeping the consumer registry current.** You should know who consumes your product and for what purpose. This isn't bureaucracy. It's what allows you to manage change responsibly and understand the full impact of decisions you make about the product.

### KPIs for Data Product Owners

Ownership should be measurable. The KPIs below provide a practical framework. Not all will apply everywhere, and thresholds should be calibrated to the maturity of the product.

**Data quality metrics:** the percentage of records meeting defined completeness, accuracy, and validity rules. These should be tracked continuously, not sampled occasionally. A declining trend is an early warning; a persistent threshold breach is an escalation trigger.

**SLA adherence:** whether the product is delivered within the timeliness and availability commitments in the contract. Late or missing data often does more damage than inaccurate data, because it blocks downstream processes entirely.

**Time to resolve data quality issues:** from the point an issue is raised to the point it's resolved. An owner who acknowledges issues and drives resolution is a different proposition from one who lets them sit in a queue.

**Consumer satisfaction:** a regular, structured measure of whether consumers feel the product is meeting their needs. It can be lightweight (a brief survey, a structured conversation) but it needs to happen consistently and the results should be visible to the owner and their management.

**Contract breach rate:** instances where the product failed to deliver what the contract promised, expressed as a rate over a rolling period. Zero is the target; the trend matters as much as the number.

**Consumer onboarding time:** how long it takes a new consumer to go from finding the product to using it successfully in production. A well-governed product with accurate documentation and a responsive owner is faster to onboard. That speed is a signal of health.

**Number of active downstream consumers:** a proxy for the product's reach and perceived value. Growing adoption suggests the product is trusted. Stagnant or declining adoption warrants investigation.

### Making ownership worth doing

The honest problem with data product ownership in most organisations is that the people being asked to own data don't benefit most from its quality. Without deliberate design, ownership will be nominal at best.

**Tie it to performance reviews.** The most direct lever. If a business owner's performance objectives include the quality metrics of the data products they own, the organisation signals that this matters. Without it, data ownership is a voluntary extra, and voluntary extras don't survive competing priorities.

**Make it visible.** Data Product Owners should be listed in the data catalogue and credited when their products enable significant outcomes. If the culture treats data ownership as invisible infrastructure, it'll attract minimal effort. If it treats it as a valued, visible contribution, it attracts better owners.

**Use show-back to make the cost of poor quality visible.** When business teams produce poor-quality data, the remediation cost lands on data engineering and downstream consumers, not on the producer. Show-back models that attribute that cost to the originating product can shift the dynamic without the friction of full charge-back. When an owner can see their product is generating significant rework cost elsewhere, the conversation about investment in quality becomes much easier.

**Align producer and consumer incentives.** Where feasible, shared metrics (where both the producing team and the consuming team are measured against the same data quality outcome) directly address the fundamental misalignment. Harder to design than individual metrics, but more powerful.

**Invest in making the role possible.** Owners disengage when the role is burdensome without being empowering. If raising a quality issue requires navigating a complex governance process with no guaranteed outcome, owners stop raising issues. If the tooling for monitoring quality is poor, they stop monitoring. Organisations that want engaged owners need to invest in the infrastructure (tooling, processes, governance authority) that makes doing the role well achievable.

## Funding data product development

One of the most persistent problems in data platforms is how development gets funded. In most cases, funding follows business deliverables. A project to deliver a risk report funds the data product needed to produce it. When the project ends, the funding stops, leaving the product in place but with no additional funding to mature it.

This promotes the production of contextual, single-purpose data products. The next project builds its own product from scratch, usually overlapping with something that already exists. The organisation ends up with many products doing similar things, built independently, maintained separately, and inconsistent with each other. Generic, reusable products (which require upfront investment with diffuse benefits) never get funded, because no single project can justify the cost.

**Consolidated consumer requirements** offer one route out. Where multiple business areas are asking for similar data, their requirements should be aggregated and presented to the data producer as a single request. Risk and Finance working together to articulate a shared data need is far more likely to result in a well-designed generic product than each team asking separately. This requires a governance function capable of identifying overlapping requirements across business lines and brokering shared solutions.

**Central investment in foundational products** offers another. Some data products are so foundational that they justify direct central investment, separate from any individual project. Party data, transaction data, and other core business entities fall into this category. Funding them centrally (with costs recovered from consumers over time) breaks the project-funding trap and allows genuinely reusable products to be built to the right quality level.

## Governance across lines of business

Risk, compliance, and finance teams typically need data that spans multiple lines of business. A risk exposure calculation might require trade data from four different business lines, each with their own platform, their own product structure, and their own ownership model.

If each line of business produces its data in a different structure, whoever aggregates it must maintain complex transformation logic for each source. The effort multiplies and so does the risk of error.

The answer is cross-line-of-business governance on data standards: someone must own the logical model that defines what goes into each product type across all lines of business, and that model must be enforced. Individual lines of business can't do this voluntarily. They have their own priorities and timelines. A central governance function with real authority has to set the standards and hold lines of business to them.

## Data platform placement and geography

As organisations adopt regionally aligned platform strategies, a new set of questions arises: which data should sit where?

This isn't purely a technical question. It has regulatory, commercial, and operational dimensions. Some data must be held within a country's borders for data residency reasons. Some must be localised to support country-specific regulatory reporting. Some is truly global and should flow through a central platform.

Getting this right requires a clear map of which data platforms exist and where they're located, which business domains and countries each platform serves, which data must be localised and which can flow freely, and where data fabric instances need to be positioned to make data accessible at both regional and global levels.

Without that map, placement decisions get made ad hoc, and data ends up in the wrong place, accessed via tactical workarounds, with the inconsistency and operational risk that brings. The cost of correcting placement decisions after data has accumulated is high. Getting ahead of this matters.

---

## Building the operating model

Ownership, governance, funding, and platform strategy all need to be codified into an operating model: a clear description of how data product development and management actually works in this organisation.

The model needs to answer some specific questions: How are data product requirements captured and prioritised? Who decides when a requirement becomes a generic product versus a contextual one? Who approves the creation of a new data product? How are breaking changes approved and communicated? Who is accountable when a data quality issue isn't resolved? How are deprecation decisions made, and who has the authority to make them? How is the consumer registry maintained?

These questions need to be answered, documented, communicated, and enforced.
