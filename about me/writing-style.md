# Writing Style Guide: How Not to Sound Like AI

This document captures instructions for writing in a way that reads as natural and human — not machine-generated. Use these rules when writing, editing, or prompting AI to write on your behalf.

---

## The core problem

AI writing tends to be grammatically correct but strategically and emotionally empty. It produces text that readers can't always explain why it feels off — but they feel it. The patterns below are what give it away. Recognising them is the first step to eliminating them.

---

## Words and phrases to avoid

These words and phrases have become so strongly associated with AI output that they should be avoided outright:

**Overused single words:** delve, realm, tapestry, landscape, leverage, harness, vibrant, crucial, compelling, nuanced, multifaceted, groundbreaking, comprehensive, robust, seamless, innovative, empower, unlock, endeavour, holistic, pivotal, paramount, transformative, actionable, cutting-edge, game-changer, deep-dive.

**Overused phrases:** "In conclusion…", "It is important to note…", "In today's fast-paced world…", "Ever-evolving landscape", "Embark on a journey", "Navigate the complexities of…", "At the end of the day", "It goes without saying", "It is worth noting that…", "A testament to…", "This isn't bureaucracy", "I hope this email finds you well."

**Hollow filler words:** very, truly, actually, certainly, absolutely, indeed, essentially, ultimately, notably.

**The negation formula:** "It's not just X, it's Y" — this contrastive structure is heavily overused by AI. Avoid it entirely.

---

## Structural patterns to avoid

**Uniform sentence length.** AI tends to write sentences that all land in the same length range. Real writing mixes short punchy sentences with longer ones. Short. Like this. And then something much longer that develops an idea and carries the reader forward before landing.

**The three-part list paragraph.** AI loves the pattern: topic sentence → three supporting points → summary sentence. Vary paragraph structure and length.

**Heavy transition words.** "Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally", "In addition to this" — these make text feel formal and mechanical. Replace with natural connectors or restructure the sentences so no connector is needed.

**Mechanical introductions and conclusions.** Opening with "This document will discuss…" or closing with "In conclusion, this paper has shown…" reads as template-driven. Start with the substance. End with a thought that has weight, not a restatement.

**Short isolated paragraphs.** A string of one-sentence or two-sentence paragraphs with no connective tissue is a tell. Ideas should flow into each other.

**No em dashes. Ever.** Do not use — in output text under any circumstances. Rewrite the sentence instead. If a parenthetical thought needs separating, use commas or brackets. If a clause needs emphasis, restructure so it stands on its own.

## What human writing does differently

**It is specific.** Replace vague adjectives with precise ones. "Effective tools" becomes "tools that reduced query time by 40%". Concrete details are what make writing believable.

**It uses "I" and "you".** When two people are talking, they use first and second person. Writing that avoids this at all costs feels corporate and evasive.

**It uses contractions.** You're, it's, we've, don't. Writing that uses the full form of every word reads as stiff and unnatural.

**It uses active voice.** "We tested the system" not "The system was tested." Active voice is shorter, clearer, and more direct.

**It varies rhythm.** Read your writing aloud. If it sounds like a TED talk or an airport business book, it needs work. Natural writing has rhythm changes — sentences that speed up, slow down, and occasionally surprise.

**It reflects a point of view.** AI summarises. Humans synthesise. The difference is whether the writing connects ideas and offers a perspective, or simply reports what exists. "These two approaches differ in X" is summary. "The difference matters because…" is synthesis.

**It shows reasoning.** Phrases like "This suggests that…" or "A likely explanation is…" or "What this means in practice is…" signal that a mind is working through a problem, not just reporting.

**It has minor imperfections.** Writing that is unnaturally perfect reads as machine-generated. A slightly unconventional sentence structure, a comma splice used deliberately, a fragment for emphasis — these are human.

## When prompting AI to write

Shallow prompts produce hollow writing. The quality of the output is determined by the quality of the context you provide.

Before asking AI to write anything, supply:
- **What the audience actually cares about** — not a label ("senior managers") but the real concerns and priorities they bring to the subject.
- **The specific point you want to make** — not just the topic but the argument, the stance, the thing you want the reader to think differently about afterwards.
- **Examples of voice** — show the AI writing you think is good. It will pattern-match on that rather than on its default output style.
- **What to avoid** — explicitly tell it which words, phrases, and structures to stay away from.

After the AI produces a draft, read it aloud. If any sentence makes you wince or sounds like a corporate press release, rewrite that sentence. The places where AI writing falls flat are usually predictable: the opening line, the transitions, and the conclusion. These are worth the most editorial attention.

---

## Quick checklist before publishing

- [ ] Read it aloud — does it sound like a person or a press release?
- [ ] Are there any banned words or phrases from the list above?
- [ ] Do all sentences have roughly the same length and structure?
- [ ] Does every paragraph follow the same topic-sentence template?
- [ ] Is there a "Furthermore", "Moreover", or "In conclusion" anywhere?
- [ ] Is there a concrete example for every abstract claim?
- [ ] Does the writing reflect a point of view, or just summarise?
- [ ] Does it open with "This document will…" or close with "In conclusion…"?
